2 · The labels

A printing error broke the link between tape labels and tape content. Can machine description measure it? sample of 5

NWFA has flagged that a printing error on the barcodes means the label information in the Heritage 2022 metadata does not match up with the file content.

The label is the only content description these 200 files have. If it cannot be trusted, the files are effectively unreachable: you cannot search them, select them for a compilation, or answer an enquiry about them. Before any of the segmentation work matters, this is the practical problem.

A description generated from the pictures and sound does not depend on the barcode at all. That is what makes the label testable: score each tape's description against the archive's own label pool, and the fit — or its absence — is a per-tape result that adds up to a measurement across the batch.

How the match is scored

For each processed Channel M tape, the pipeline's description of it — written from the pictures and sound; the pipeline never saw the label — is embedded together with the archive's full H22 label list (around 3,000 labels, not just this batch's 200) using a sentence-embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2). The five nearest labels are kept, and two numbers come out per tape: how similar the best candidate is (%match1), and how far it clears the runner-up (simDif). The spreadsheet colours each row from those two:

  • Green — best candidate above 60% similar with more than 10 points on the runner-up: a confident, unambiguous label for the tape.
  • Yellow — best candidate above 50%, or more than 5 points on the runner-up: a plausible label, but an ambiguous one.
  • Blank — no candidate clears the bar: the pool offers nothing the description fits.

The five tapes NWFA has judged independently check the heuristic. None of the four whose labels do not match the content — all five annotators said no on each — scores green: two find no candidate at all, and two top out on a pool of near-identical "Channel M News" labels, tied with zero margin. The contested tape does score green: its best candidate is a 4 Manchester label — the same programme its own spine label names, just not that exact one.

Because the candidates come from the whole H22 label list rather than this batch's 200 labels, a confident match can point at a label that is currently sitting on a different tape. That is the re-pairing opportunity: instead of correcting labels one at a time, the machine descriptions could be used to match labels to files across the batch.

TaskChannel MFilm & Video
Outer segmentation Nothing. No duration, no programme count, no content description. The label sometimes names more than one item, but it cannot be relied on to name even one correctly. An assumption. One catalogue record per film, so the working assumption is one file = one programme — but compilation reels break it, and our sample already contains one that does.
Inner segmentation Nothing. Prose only. The synopsis describes the film shot by shot in words at the median, with no timecodes anywhere, so it can support a judgement about what the parts are but not where they are.
Descriptive metadata Nothing beyond the label. Strong. Year, colour, sound, duration, producer, place and index terms on essentially every record.
The rushes problem. Most of Channel M is rushes and raw footage rather than transmitted programmes, and "programme" may be the wrong unit for it. One sampled tape is a reporter recording the same piece-to-camera over and over; the pipeline described it as a comic sketch about a reporter endlessly re-running his report, which is a fair account of what is on the screen. Whether NWFA wants the story (takes collapsed) or the take (takes separated) changes the output completely.

The detector is not at fault: it found the bars correctly. The problem is that when two separators sit close together, the sliver between them is emitted as a clip and then dutifully described — the most complex tape, the Channel 4 Manchester magazine, carries three such slivers, five to six seconds each, each sitting between two detected separators.

The second pass's programme flag now marks most of these itself: programmes are flagged false — of them inside the heuristic's list, beyond it (plus with no second-pass data). The flag is the primary signal; the duration rule remains a cheap safety net, since short clips bounded by separators on both sides — of the here — can be dropped immediately even without the model.

One confabulation in the ten-file working sample — legible, and fixed by the prompt. A Film & Video amateur reel, titled in the catalogue "Playing bridge at the Calf's Head Hotel; trip to Knaresborough and Brimham Rocks" — both inland (Worston, Lancashire; the Yorkshire Dales) — came back in the first run placed in "Cornwall": the catalogue's Brimham Rocks boulders had become "the dramatic wind-carved stacks and boulders of the Calf's Head coastline". The model appears to have mapped the hotel's sign onto the famous Cornish headland of the same name. It is wrong in a way an archivist spots instantly, and in a way the catalogue would have prevented if the pipeline had been given it. The re-pointed places prompt — a toponym only if certain, and seen on a sign or in subtitles — has removed it since: in the full run the same footage comes back as "Calf's Head", environment "outdoor countryside", with no coastline.
7 · Vocabularies

The machine now works in NWFA's own vocabulary

For the full run the prompt asks directly for NWFA's two controlled lists — the nine-term category and the ten-term category2 — so the output arrives in the archive's own language rather than as a parallel description. The question has changed from "do the two vocabularies touch?" to "how well does the machine's use of them line up with the catalogue's?"

Machine types

NWFA's two lists, as asked by the prompt — across all proposed programmes

One field, two axes: category (subject) and category2 (provenance — how and why the film was made). Eighteen of the nineteen terms came back; Scientific never did.

NWFA category

Nine terms, multi-valued, across the 200 Film & Video records

Channel M was never catalogued, so its programmes have no catalogue terms to agree with — the machine's terms are all they have.

Subject: of films agree. On of the Film & Video files that carry a catalogue category, the machine's terms overlap it — most often on Sport and leisure, Culture and traditions and Travel. Term for term the two agree exactly on of the ; on the machine covers everything the catalogue says, usually with additions — it is generous, not precise. The lopsided term is Culture and traditions, added by the machine on 39 files against 7 the other way round.

Provenance: same concept, different labels

The second list — category2, the Professional and Amateur sub-categories — is where the mapping gets harder: on raw labels only of films share a sub-category with the catalogue. Part of the gap is a naming difference: the catalogue files its amateur material under Special Interest ( films) where the machine says Amateur or Home Movies ( films), and reading those labels as the same term lifts the overlap to . The rest is genuine disagreement about which film gets which sub-category — both sides reach for Factual Record and Local Cinema Topicals a great deal, on different films.

10 · Findings

What we now know, what we could know with more development, and what needs a human first

What we know

  • Machine description can be compared against the Channel M labels without depending on the barcode, so the mismatch is measurable.
  • Separator detection is working. separators across files, median s, and tape leader is being found where you would expect it.
  • Coverage is high — median of each file is accounted for by proposed programmes.
  • Film & Video files mostly resolve to one programme, and the one compilation reel in the sample was correctly split into four.
  • Prose fields are always returned; evidential fields — title, year, credits — are returned only when stated on screen.

What we could know

  • Whether machine output can be filed. Re-pointing the prompt at NWFA's category, Professional/Amateur and sub-category vocabularies is a prompt change, not a model change.
  • Whether the duration discrepancy is a knowable transfer-speed rule, which would turn it into an automatic check.
  • Whether the music content — early TV performances by named bands — supports performer identification, which is a different task with different risks.

What needs a human first

  • What counts as a programme in rushes.
  • The label-match verdicts. Only an archivist can assign them, and they are the headline measurement.
  • Which vocabularies the machine should target, and whether machine terms sit in the same FileMaker fields as human ones or alongside them.
  • The transfer-speed rule behind the duration disagreement.
  • The tolerance for confabulation, which sets how aggressively the prompt can be pushed.
11 · For discussion

Six questions for the workshop.

What is a programme in a rushes tape?

Most of Channel M is rushes, and the machine is already showing us the problem: one sampled tape is a reporter re-recording the same piece-to-camera repeatedly, described by the pipeline as a comic sketch about exactly that. It is not wrong about what it sees.

Is the unit NWFA wants the story, with all takes collapsed, or the take, separated — which is what an editor reusing footage would want?

Decision: the unit of outer segmentation, before scale-up.

Is the barcode error systematic or random?

If the printing error is an offset — labels shifted by one, or one crate's labels transposed with another's — machine descriptions across all 200 files could let us re-pair labels to files rather than correcting them one at a time.

Five tapes is too small to tell. Does the error have a shape Nick already knows about? The batch has two crates, which is the obvious first thing to test.

Decision: whether re-pairing is in scope for the 400-run.

Which vocabularies should the machine target?

NWFA has three: category (nine subject terms), the Professional/Amateur split with its sub-categories, and free-text index terms at terms per record. The current prompt points at none of them.

We can target one or two well, not all three at once. And in FileMaker, do machine terms share fields with human ones or sit alongside them?

Decision: the target vocabulary and the field layout for ingest.

What should the validation sample cover?

The two batches need opposite things. For Film & Video, year, colour, sound and title are already catalogued; asking again spends the budget on nothing. For Channel M the catalogue is empty and one tick-box per tape produces the project's headline metric.

Proposal: label-match, programme count and timecodes for Channel M; programme count, timecodes and a verdict on the machine text for Film & Video. How many tapes is that in practice?

Decision: the annotation template and its size.
