2 · The labels

A printing error broke the link between tape labels and tape content. Can machine description measure it? sample of 5

NWFA has flagged that a printing error on the barcodes means the label information in the Heritage 2022 metadata does not match up with the file content.

The label is the only content description these 200 files have. If it cannot be trusted, the files are effectively unreachable: you cannot search them, select them for a compilation, or answer an enquiry about them. Before any of the segmentation work matters, this is the practical problem.

A description generated from the pictures and sound does not depend on the barcode at all, so comparing it against the label gives a per-tape verdict — and across tapes, a measurement.

"AI recovers an access layer that a printing error took away", where the alternative is 200 mislabelled tapes. The verdicts in that table are cheap for an archivist to assign (one judgement per tape, no timecoding), and they scale to a number that can be reported.
If the printing error has a shape — labels offset by one, or two crates transposed — then machine descriptions across all files could let us re-pair labels to files rather than just flagging the mismatches. That would be a far better outcome than correcting them one at a time, and it is only testable at scale. The two crates in this batch (—) are an obvious thing to check first.

What we have to compare against, per task

TaskChannel MFilm & Video
Outer segmentation Nothing. No duration, no programme count, no content description. The label sometimes names more than one item, but it cannot be relied on to name even one correctly. An assumption. One catalogue record per film, so the working assumption is one file = one programme — but compilation reels break it, and our sample already contains one that does.
Inner segmentation Nothing. Prose only. The synopsis describes the film shot by shot in words at the median, with no timecodes anywhere, so it can support a judgement about what the parts are but not where they are.
Descriptive metadata Nothing beyond the label. Strong. Year, colour, sound, duration, producer, place and index terms on essentially every record.
The rushes problem. Most of Channel M is rushes and raw footage rather than transmitted programmes, and "programme" may be the wrong unit for it. One sampled tape is a reporter recording the same piece-to-camera over and over; the pipeline described it as a comic sketch about a reporter endlessly re-running his report, which is a fair account of what is on the screen. Whether NWFA wants the story (takes collapsed) or the take (takes separated) changes the output completely.

List every full-screen separator between distinct programmes. A visual separator usually looks like
SMPTE color bars, blank screen, production card, countdown leaders, static or white noise. It is

The Film & Video reel shows a genuine compilation — four distinct wartime films on one reel, correctly separated.

Finding: separator residue is being emitted as programmes. of the proposed programmes () contain no programme content — colour bars, blank screen, a grey frame. Median duration second. The detector is not at fault: it found the bars correctly. The problem is that when two separators sit close together, the sliver between them is emitted as a clip and then dutifully described. On the long Channel M tape, a separator at 52:21 is followed by a 1-second clip and then another separator at 52:32.

Because the clip prompt asks for types unconditionally, the model assigns a genre to colour bars: —. Nothing in the output marks these rows as non-content, so they would flow straight into any downstream index.

The fix might not need the model. Dropping or flagging clips below about five seconds that are bounded by separators on both sides removes of the immediately; a non-content value in types would catch the rest and keep them auditable rather than silently deleted.

One confabulation in ten files, and it is legible. A Film & Video amateur reel titled in the catalogue as a trip to Knaresborough and Brimham Rocks — inland Yorkshire — came back placed in "Cornwall", with invented sea stacks and coastline in the synopsis. 10 · Findings

What we now know, what we could know with more development, and what needs a human first

What we know

  • Machine description can be compared against the Channel M labels without depending on the barcode, so the mismatch is measurable rather than merely known.
  • Separator detection is working. separators across files, median s, and tape leader is being found where you would expect it.
  • Coverage is high — median of each file is accounted for by proposed programmes.
  • Film & Video files mostly resolve to one programme, and the one compilation reel in the sample was correctly split into four.
  • Prose fields are always returned; evidential fields — title, year, credits — are returned only when stated on screen.

What we could know

  • Whether the barcode error has a shape. Testable across all Channel M files, and if it does, labels could be re-paired rather than just corrected.
  • Whether machine output can be filed. Re-pointing the prompt at NWFA's category, Professional/Amateur and sub-category vocabularies is a prompt change, not a model change.
  • Whether the duration discrepancy is a knowable transfer-speed rule, which would turn it into an automatic check.
  • Whether the music content — early TV performances by named bands — supports performer identification, which is a different task with different risks.

What needs a human first

  • What counts as a programme in rushes. Story or take. Nothing downstream can be settled before this.
  • The label-match verdicts. Only an archivist can assign them, and they are the headline measurement.
  • Which vocabularies the machine should target, and whether machine terms sit in the same FileMaker fields as human ones or alongside them.
  • The transfer-speed rule behind the duration disagreement.
  • The tolerance for confabulation, which sets how aggressively the prompt can be pushed.
11 · For discussion

Six questions

What is a programme in a rushes tape?

Most of Channel M is rushes, and the machine is already showing us the problem: one sampled tape is a reporter re-recording the same piece-to-camera repeatedly, described by the pipeline as a comic sketch about exactly that. It is not wrong about what it sees.

Is the unit NWFA wants the story, with all takes collapsed, or the take, separated — which is what an editor reusing footage would want?

Decision: the unit of outer segmentation, before scale-up.

Is the barcode error systematic or random?

If the printing error is an offset — labels shifted by one, or one crate's labels transposed with another's — machine descriptions across all 200 files could let us re-pair labels to files rather than correcting them one at a time.

Five tapes is too small to tell. Does the error have a shape Nick already knows about? The batch has two crates, which is the obvious first thing to test.

Decision: whether re-pairing is in scope for the 400-run.

Which vocabularies should the machine target?

NWFA has three: category (nine subject terms), the Professional/Amateur split with its sub-categories, and free-text index terms at terms per record. The current prompt points at none of them.

We can target one or two well, not all three at once. And in FileMaker, do machine terms share fields with human ones or sit alongside them?

Decision: the target vocabulary and the field layout for ingest.

What should the validation sample cover?

The two batches need opposite things. For Film & Video, year, colour, sound and title are already catalogued; asking again spends the budget on nothing. For Channel M the catalogue is empty and one tick-box per tape produces the project's headline metric.

Proposal: label-match, programme count and timecodes for Channel M; programme count, timecodes and a verdict on the machine text for Film & Video. How many tapes is that in practice?

Decision: the annotation template and its size.
